## Part 1: Universal Mechanics Description Framework (slides 4-46)

**Mechanics X-ray stepper** (slides 20-33, reused on 52-54, 100, 104-107), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: every mechanic breaks down into States (HP, a torch lit or unlit), Signals (messages that ask a system to do something), Receivers (entities that accept certain kinds of signals), Transforms (changes in state) and Context (extra information attached to a signal, such as the school of damage, the amount and whether it crit, per the notes on 104). Slides 28-33 X-ray Mario 3. The notes spell out the signal types: Jump and Shoot are command signals from the player, Stomp and Hit are side-effect signals from Mario's feet or the fireball's impact, then damage and experience signals pass to the health and level-up systems. The fireball "moves on its own with its own attack zone"; a Goomba is either stomped or hit; at 0 health it sends a death signal internally, which triggers the reward and animation systems and releases XP.
- Hard as text: the notes themselves say "this slide is a little high concept" and "don't worry if you didn't follow all of this". The slides add one box at a time across 14 slides, but the point is the flow: a signal leaves one entity, a receiver accepts it, a transform changes a state, and that change fires the next signal. The green waves on slide 28 are signals, which is a motion idea drawn as a still.
- Proposal: a two-layer view. Top: a small original platformer scene (a hero, a walking enemy). Bottom: the X-ray diagram from slides 29-33. Press Jump or Shoot and a glowing signal packet travels along the diagram's arrows, coloured by kind (command, side effect, system). Each receiver lights up as it accepts, the state value ticks (1 to 0, 0 to 100 XP), and the next system fires. A "step" button walks one hop at a time so the instructor can narrate. A legend colours each box by its framework part. The same engine then drives the one-time switch (52-54: Inactive, "Lever tool used", Set Active, Active), the flammable grass (100: notes say an arrow or torch sends a burn signal, anything flammable changes from stable to on fire, and the fire spreads to other flammable objects) and type weakness (107), so the framework shows up as one tool used four times. GIF per scene.

**Candle thermometer** (slides 20-25), effort **S**, can be the first screen of the X-ray stepper
- Teaches: the five parts built up on one tiny example. The notes name it: a candle you can heat with fire or cool with frost magic, where the temperature decides whether it is lit.
- Proposal: a candle with a temperature state of 5 and a lit threshold. Fire and Frost buttons send Heat and Cool signals. Toggle the candle's Warmable and Coolable receivers off and the matching signal bounces off with no change. Toggle the transform between +1 and +2. A context chip (for example "strong fire") changes the transform. Each part's label highlights as it is used, in the deck's order, and the flame lights or goes out when the state crosses the threshold.

**Mechanic or aesthetic? Cutting grass** (slide 18), effort **S**, quick win
- Teaches: if it doesn't leave a significant gameplay change on the world, it's aesthetic, not a mechanic. The notes use Zelda grass that sometimes reveals treasure vs grass that is cut down with no effect, and stress both have their place.
- Proposal: one patch of grass and a sword. Toggle "mechanic" off and slashes cut the grass with particles but nothing else; toggle it on and some tufts drop a rupee or reveal a hole. A label under the scene reads out which one you are looking at and why. GIF of both.

**Leveling system cascade** (slides 14-15), effort **S**
- Teaches: systems create side effects. Killing enemies gives XP, XP raises Level, Level raises HP and MP, so the player can take more hits and cast more spells. The notes add the key point: the player cannot turn the XP dial directly (unless cheating); they have to deal damage or finish quests.
- Proposal: the slide's dial picture as linked gears: XP turns Level, Level turns HP and MP. The XP gear has no handle; the only way to turn it is to defeat an enemy in a tiny scene above. On each level-up, a short test fight replays showing one more hit survived and one more spell cast. GIF.

**Parts vs verbs vs mechanics sorter** (slide 40), effort **S**
- Teaches: the thing used to deliver a mechanic is not the mechanic. Notes: dice are rolled to make a number, but the mechanic is uncertainty; dice, a deck of cards or a random number generator are all just different forms of uncertainty.
- Hard as text: the slide shows the answer, so learners never test their own instinct, and the notes warn that other classes get this wrong.
- Proposal: a drag-to-sort quiz with three bins and a dozen chips (the slide's words plus a few extra like "spinner", "drawing", "hidden information"). A second round shows three randomizers (die, shuffled deck, RNG) producing the same uncertainty, to land the notes' point. Wrong drops explain why.

## Part 2: 6 Qualities of Deep Mechanics and Player Tools (slides 47-77)

**The guarded door: key, lockpick, hammer** (slides 58-63, with the tool matrix on 83-90 and the reminder on 190), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: the notes build one example across slides 58-63. A key is the weakest tool: one key, one lock. A lockpick works on door locks and chest locks without ever seeing the key. A hammer smashes doors and chests and fights enemies. Then a security guard watches the door, and each tool gets a different consequence: the key lets you straight in; the lockpick makes the guard suspicious and interrogate you, so you must pass or distract him first; the hammer makes him fire at you openly, so you must defeat him first. Three real choices with visible, strong consequences. Part 3 turns this into the tool matrix: narrow vs broad (one key and one door vs one lockpick for three doors, 86) and shallow vs versatile (keys and lockpicks only remove locks; the Ocarina hammer is a weapon, breaks barriers, slams rusty switches and breaks shields, 87). Slide 88 places Zelda boss keys (narrow, shallow), silver keys, Skyrim lockpicks (broad, shallow) and the hammer (broad, versatile), and says to focus on broad and versatile mechanics first. Slide 90: 80/20 it, 80% simple, 20% broad and versatile. Slide 83 shows Moon Studios' Ori 2 sheet of how each weapon, skill and spell was expected to affect each monster. Slide 190: one tool per context is just lock and key again.
- Hard as text: the guard example is told across six slides in the notes only; the slides themselves show tool icons and "+" and "=". It is a perfect small scene, and it is invisible unless the instructor reads it out.
- Proposal, part one: a room with a door, a chest and an enemy, and a toolbar of key, lockpick and hammer. Use a tool on each target and a grid fills in (tools as rows, targets as columns), so the key lights one cell and the hammer lights a row. A "guard" toggle adds the security guard and plays the notes' three consequences when you use each tool on the door. Part two: the grid feeds the narrow/broad x shallow/versatile chart from slide 88, with the slide's four examples placed as reference and the learner's own tools dropped in. A cost meter adds $ per tool, $$ per situation and $$$ per thing that must be taught (slide 74: cost scales with the number of tools), and an 80/20 bar shows what share of the kit is core. A last view shows an Ori-style tool x monster sheet (original monsters) with hits to kill per cell, to show the planning step from slide 83.

**Innate, receiver, tool** (slides 49-51), effort **S**
- Teaches: some mechanics are always available (Mario jumping or moving), some only when a device exposes them (a switch or handle), and some only when both the right situation and the right tool are in hand (the rusted switch needs the hammer).
- Proposal: three short GIFs with one original hero: walks and jumps; steps on a switch and it clicks; steps on a rusted switch, nothing, equips the hammer, it clicks. Pairs with the X-ray stepper's one-time switch scene (52-54).

**Simple environment, more choices: Portal vs Portal 2** (slides 76-77), effort **S-M**
- Teaches: more environments do not mean more choices. The notes compare Portal 1's many white surfaces, which allow creative problem solving, with Portal 2's more constrained spaces, which make a more linear, directed experience. The instructor note asks for "one sentence: too constrained, one solution, less creativity".
- Proposal: one original test chamber drawn twice with the same exit. In the first, most walls take portals; in the second, only two marked panels do and the rest is detailed but inert. A route overlay draws every valid path and counts them. The second room looks richer and has one answer. GIF.

**One-use keys vs multi-purpose items** (slides 68, 73), effort **S**, low priority
- Teaches: notes on 73: multi-purpose items have more value to the player on a regular basis, while one-use keys (Link's Awakening and Ocarina trading sequences) are really just visual markers of progress.
- Proposal: an inventory over a play session, where each item's icon glows each time it is used. Multi-purpose items glow all session; trading-sequence items glow once and then sit there. Could be a still or short GIF.

The multi-purpose examples on 69-71 (Yoshi egg toss, Ori Bash, TF2 rocket launcher) already have YouTube clips in the notes, and slide 69's note asks for several clips back to back covering all four situations. If those slides still need footage, a video wall like `pvp/bruiser-meta` with timestamped clips is the cheapest fix. No original animation is proposed for them.

## Part 4: 6 Techniques to Help Players Master Your Mechanics (slides 94-144)

**One mechanic, six ways to teach it** (slides 97-132), effort **L**, high priority (just outside the top 5)
- Teaches: Accidental Discovery (BotW fire on grass "by accident", Half-Life 2 saw blade timed with an enemy, Pokemon type weakness), Demonstration (Mirror's Edge's Celeste, Mega Man X's Zero showing the charge shot), Nuzzles (the simplest possible use of a mechanic, set up so you can't progress until you do it: the Deku Tree ladder, the torch, God of War's shielded Olympic Guardians), Tutorialization (exact steps, with the notes' advice to let experienced players skip), Drip Exposure (LoL and WoW unlocks, the Devil May Cry shop, LoR chunks) and Free Practice (SFV, LoL practice tool, Control). Bonus: intentionally don't teach, where the notes contrast Dark Souls 3's kick (only mentioned in messages, tricky input, many players never learn it) with poise (it just makes sense that a big metal guy doesn't flinch unless hit with a huge hammer).
- Hard as text: six names with game logos. Novices can't compare them because every example teaches a different thing. Most of these slides carry video in the notes, which shows each game but not the comparison.
- Proposal: hold the mechanic constant (fire spreads through grass, matching the notes' BotW example) and show six tiny original level vignettes, one per technique: the player swings a torch at an enemy standing in grass and it catches; an NPC burns a bush ahead of them; a dead end with only grass, a torch and a brazier; a prompt "press X to light"; fire arrows unlocked in a later level; a practice field with infinite fire. A seventh, "don't teach", shows the kick problem: a shield enemy and a hidden input most players never find. Each vignette is a GIF, and a page shows all of them in a grid with "player effort" and "designer control" readouts.

**Type weakness X-ray** (slides 104-107), effort **S**, quick win
- Teaches: notes on 107: the damage signal arrives with a context, which results in a different transformation on the Pokemon's health, so it ends up either dying or just wounded. 10 HP, a 5 damage hit, x2 if the damage is electric and the target is electric vulnerable.
- Proposal: a target with toggles for damage type (electric, fire, physical), crit, and "electric vulnerable". Fire the hit and the X-ray boxes from slide 107 light in order, with the context bundle shown as a tag riding on the signal (the notes on 104 list school, amount and crit). Uses the X-ray stepper's renderer.

**Anti-patterns** (slides 133-136), effort **S** each, medium priority
- Teaches: the notes name all three. Overteaching: DOOM Eternal's weak point prompts made many players think the game was only about weak points, when shooting them is a tactical choice that kills slower but disables the enemy's weapons. Anti-lessons: Super Mario Bros taught that mushrooms help, then the Japanese Super Mario Bros 2 added poison mushrooms. Hinting to overcome design: Resident Evil 4's interactables didn't look different from the scenery, so yellow X sprays were added late in development.
- Hard as text: the slides are title-only, so the examples live entirely in the notes.
- Proposal: three short original GIFs with original art: an enemy with a glowing weak point and a prompt that keeps nagging, then a side-by-side showing "shoot the weak point" (slower, enemy disarmed) vs "shoot the body" (faster kill) so the trade-off the prompt hides is visible; a power-up that heals in level 1 and an identical-looking one that hurts in level 2; a room where the breakable crates look like the scenery, then the same room with spray-painted X marks.

**How much to teach: audience slider** (slides 137-142), effort **S**, low priority
- Teaches: tailor teaching to the audience through UX, playtesting and iteration. Someone's very first game needs everything explained; someone's favourite genre in a new flavour should be set free. Notes on 140: playtesting finds the balance between over and under teaching.
- Proposal: a slider from "first game ever" to "genre veteran" that turns tutorial prompts, demonstrations and free practice up or down on a mock first level.

## Part 5: 4 Key Contexts That Enhance Player Satisfaction (slides 147-191)

**Context web: one ability, four context layers** (slides 153-190), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: the more contextually fitting a mechanic is, the more fun and satisfying it is (149). Slide 153's "Walls vs Webs" is explained in the notes: if you've tried to set a castle wall on fire and nothing happened while the spider webs on it burned instantly, you understand context. The four contexts are Environment, Opponent, Ally and Self. Notes on 156 give the rule for the whole part: contexts must warp the way you approach a situation, otherwise why have them (the BotW storm makes metal gear dangerous and wooden gear essential). Opponent examples: throws beat blocks (160), counter hits (161), Sekiro's bull dazed by a wall (162), God Hand dizzy finishers (163), Nioh enemy stamina (164). Ally: Half-Life scientists heal you (166), Genshin's Wet status makes electricity arc a second time and makes freezing easier (167). Self: full meter or transformation (169), long like demon armor or short like rogue evasion (170). Combinations: notes on 187 say Hades pushback against walls deals bonus damage, and enemies that run into walls get stunned. Magicka is the rare all-four example (189). Slide 190: a tool should have many hooks across contexts, otherwise it is a shallow lock and key again.
- Hard as text: the combination slides are a list of game names. The "web" only shows up when you add a context layer and watch the outcomes multiply.
- Proposal: a small top-down arena and one ability, a knockback shove (the Hades example). Toggle each context layer on: Environment adds walls, spikes and a ledge (shove into a wall deals bonus damage, into spikes damages, off a ledge kills); Opponent adds a blocking enemy the shove breaks through and a charging enemy that stuns itself on a wall; Ally adds a partner who soaks enemies (Wet) so the shove into a puddle chains a second hit; Self adds a meter that fills on each wall slam and makes the next shove bigger. A side panel draws the web: the ability in the centre, a strand to each hook it can use, and a counter of distinct outcomes (one layer alone vs all four). An opening "Walls vs Webs" beat uses the notes' own picture: a fire bolt does nothing to a castle wall and burns the webs on it. GIF tours the layers one by one.

**Hooks and how much snapping** (slides 176-184), effort **S-M**
- Teaches: hooks are visual anchors that say "this can interact" (176-178, named after the hookshot's target symbols, needed in 3D where "shoot and try" is too broad). They can be environment cues or animation cues (Sekiro's dive over water, 180), subtle or bold (Mirror's Edge's red runner vision, 183). Notes on 182 add a caution: overly aggressive detection makes the character feel like it snaps between interaction points instead of being controlled by the player.
- Proposal: one original side-view level. A first toggle fades in an outline on every hook, tagged by the ability that uses it. A second control is the detection radius for ledge grab and cover: small and the player misses ledges; large and the character visibly snaps across gaps on its own. A "who is driving?" readout shows how much of the path came from the player vs from snapping.

## Part 6: How Mechanics Impact Resources and Resource Flow (slides 195-259)

**Cast timeline: Life Tap, class imbalance, tokens and the weakness window** (slides 225-256), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: the case study from the instructor's own WoW work. Notes on 225: WoW casters fail unless they press a damage button every single global cooldown, because mana is a full pool meant to burn out over minutes, so any pause is lost damage; the instructor woke up with aching hands after raiding. Life Tap traded health for mana (notes on 226: say 10% health for 10% mana), a real risk solo (227) and meaningless in raids (228). Time is a resource: four Fireballs fit in the window back to back, three if you take breaks, which doesn't matter solo and matters a lot with 39 others counting on you (233-237). Mage casts 4 Fireballs for 10; Warlock casts 3 Shadow Bolts for 13 plus a Life Tap, "close enough" (239-244). Then Burning Crusade added mana restoration for allies, Life Tap stopped being needed, and Warlocks did 30% more damage (245). The fix: a token per Shadow Bolt, 4 tokens at 25 damage = 100 per window (248-249), shorter casts at the same total (250-252), back to back for burst but the same average because tokens only return every 10 seconds (253-254). The payoff on 255: a boss with a weakness window. In the locked system the Warlock gets the same bonus whatever they do; with tokens they save up and land 3 blasts in the window instead of 2. "Using resources creates freedom for the player." Destruction got Chaotic Energy in place of Life Tap (256).
- Hard as text: the deck spends about 30 build-up slides on one timeline, and the most important beat (the weakness window) is only in the notes. The ideas are rates and sequences, and a learner who misses one slide loses the thread.
- Proposal: two cast bars over the 10-second window (mage, warlock) that the learner fills by clicking spells, with the slides' damage numbers and running totals. A mana bar forces Life Tap when empty, and a "Burning Crusade" toggle adds ally mana restoration so the learner watches the Warlock jump to 52 vs 40. A "take a break" button inserts a pause and shows the lost cast. Tab two applies the token redesign step by step (4 tokens, 25 damage, shorter casts, back to back), with a button-presses-per-window counter that falls while total damage stays at 100. Tab three adds the boss weakness window as a highlighted band that moves each run: in the locked system the damage inside it never changes; with tokens the learner saves casts and lands 3 in it. Numbers come straight from the slides and notes; cast times are read off the slide timeline. GIF per stage.

**Resource pool sandbox** (slides 198-216, 257), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: a resource is a pool that can be accumulated, capped and spent (199), from mana to gold to Kratos' rage (200). Its three parts are generators, spenders and capacity, and notes on 202 say inflow and outflow matter most and must balance or neither matters. Generators: passive (mana and stamina in most action games), active (potions, skills), conversion (health into mana, later Soul Shards), conditional (crits give 50% more resource) (204-207). Outflow is what you spend plus optional equilibrium points where the pool decays to a threshold (208-209). Spenders: active, passive drain (rage lost out of combat urges you to use it while you have it, 211), triggered (poise: consumed when struck, at zero you stagger, 212), theft (pickpocketing, mana burn, "dangerous in PvP", 213). Discrete vs continuous (215-216): grenades in a clip and Jhin's four bullets (every 4th shot hits harder, then he must reload) vs a battery, the Subnautica Seaglide, or a spell costing 2% mana per second. Slide 257: explore the extremes by doubling or halving capacity, inflow and outflow.
- Hard as text: flow is a rate over time, and the slides show it as a still bar (MP 75/100). Novices can't feel how changing one rate reshapes play.
- Proposal: a pool with inflow pipes and outflow drains, each a toggleable generator or spender from the notes' lists (passive regen, a potion button, convert HP to resource, +50% on crit; casting, out-of-combat decay, poise break on hit, an enemy that steals). A capacity slider and a decay threshold for equilibrium. A live graph of the pool over 30 seconds, plus a "can I act?" strip that shows when the player is blocked. Slide 257's six buttons (x2 and x½ for capacity, inflow, outflow) jump straight to the extremes. Presets: action-game stamina, rage that drains out of combat, poise, Jhin's four-shot clip, Seaglide battery.

**Life Tap solo vs raid** (slides 226-228), effort **S**, quick win
- Proposal: a GIF of the slide's three bars (Priest, Warlock, Boss). Solo: the warlock taps, a monster is on them, and the next hit is a threat. Raid: the warlock taps, the priest heals it back, "THX, BRO~ / LOL!", and they tap again. Can be cut from the cast timeline page.

**Discrete vs continuous spending** (slides 215-216), effort **S**
- Proposal: Jhin-style four-shot counter (4th shot flashes bigger, then a reload) next to a battery bar draining while a speed boost is held. One looping GIF. Also a preset in the pool sandbox.

**Threshold states: full-health sword beam** (slides 219-220), effort **S**
- Teaches: notes on 220: in Zelda 1, A Link to the Past and A Link Between Worlds, the sword fires beams while health is full, giving extra range, but only while you take no damage at all.
- Proposal: an HP bar and a sword. At full HP each swing fires a beam; take one hit and the beams stop until healed. GIF.

**Unlimited resources and the flow channel** (slides 222-224), effort **S**, low priority
- Teaches: resources exist to limit player actions. Notes on 224: with unlimited resources the dominant strategy ignores attrition and there is no reason to improve, while a meter that allows a brief rampage lets the player choose when and where to be overpowered.
- Proposal: the slide's skill vs challenge chart with a dot for the player. A slider removes the limit and the dot slides into the boring zone; a "rampage meter" mode brings it back into the channel with a short spike the player triggers.

**Positive feedback loop** (slide 258), effort **S**, low priority
- Teaches: converting resources both ways can make a positive feedback loop. Notes: these are fun in short experiences but can break even expensive AAA games, naming Diablo 3's lifesteal.
- Proposal: the slide's music loop as an animated cycle where each lap grows the arrows, then a toggle that applies the same loop to a lifesteal build (more damage heals more, which lets you stand in more damage) until the challenge line flattens out.
