# Module 0: The Power of Polish: interactive and GIF proposals

Source: "RGF C2 M0 - The Power of Polish" (Drive 1P753uljkYe6G-m008fjaLA3jxRiYXGtU6poHjI8N38g, 90 slides), the opening deck of *Directing Game Mechanics Satisfaction* (formerly *Refining Gameplay and Feel*). First pass 2026-11-04 from the Drive text export with speaker notes. Slide numbers are the export's "N of 90" numbers. Quotes marked "notes" come from the speaker notes.

Built so far: nothing yet. The course home is `../index.html`.

This is an orientation deck: half of it is one-line statement slides (4-34, 79-90) and a contributor roll call (35-47). The interactive value is in Part 3 (slides 48-78), which previews every later module. Most proposals here are therefore small, and two of them (the course map and the feel teaser) are built to be reused by later modules.

Effort: **S** = one GIF or a single small widget (under a day), **M** = a page with a few controls or a reusable rules table (1-2 days), **L** = a sandbox with several modes (3+ days).

## Part 1: Why are you here? (slides 3-34)

**From designer to director** (slides 4-17), effort **S**, GIF, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: "Designers need to interface with many other roles in the studio" (6, 7), "in small projects those roles are all the same person… or just you!" (8), so "a designer is like a Junior Director" (10). Direction becomes more critical as a designer becomes more senior (16), and indie designers are frequently also the directors (17).
- Hard as text: fourteen statement slides make one point. A picture makes it in five seconds.
- Proposal: a designer token in the middle. Role badges from slide 7 (Programmer, Animator, Environment Artist, Character Designer, Level Designer, Balancer) fly in around it and connect with lines; the token grows a director's cap as the lines multiply. Second beat, "small project": the badges slide inward and stack on the one token until a single person carries all six. Role icons from game-icons.net.

**"It depends"** (slides 27-29), effort **S**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: "when you ask us, 'Is thing X good or bad?' We're probably going to tell you, 'It depends.' We can give you the tools to help you figure out when [it fits], but we can't answer that for you."
- Hard as text: "it depends" sounds like a dodge until a learner sees the same mechanic flip.
- Proposal: pick a mechanic (permadeath, auto-aim, fall damage, fast travel) and watch it placed into three real games, each with a one-line reason: for example, permadeath in *Hades* (the run is the loop), in *Pokémon* Nuzlocke rules (a player-chosen challenge) and in *Animal Crossing* (it would erase the reason to play). Each verdict reads **fits the pillars** or **fights the pillars**, never "good" or "bad". Ends on the slide 29 line and a pointer to Module 1, since pillars are the tool that answers "depends on what?".
- Note: the slide text itself uses "good or bad". The visual should show that phrase only as the learner's question and use "fits / fights" for every verdict.

**The toolbox fills up** (slides 22-26), effort **S**, GIF (quick win, see above)

**What this course is not** (slides 30-34), effort **S**, low priority
- Teaches: production, marketing, coding and tooling, and leadership are out of scope; the course covers "the content of the game itself".
- Proposal: a two-column sort, "in this course" vs "learn it elsewhere", with the four topics dropping into the right column and the module names into the left. Small enough to fold into the course map page as a footer.

## Part 3: What does game direction consist of? (slides 48-78)

**Course map wheel** (slides 54-58), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: slides 54-55 ring "Game Mechanics & Experience" with Player Profiling, Skill Testing & Progression, Character Design, Camera Immersion, Responsive Controller Design, Gameplay Balance and VFX & SFX Feedback; notes: "7 building blocks, spread across 7 modules." Slide 56 groups them into "3 Phases of Crafting Emotionally Engaging Mechanics", and its notes define each: Insight is "the 'why', everything that informs design decisions before a single line of code is written" (audience, fantasy, balance); the 3Cs are "the interactive core, the moment-to-moment 'how'"; Readability is "the 'does it land?' pillar" (visual clarity, audio language, test design, information architecture). Notes on 57-58 give the order to tackle them: identify your target audience, decide on skill level and tests, develop a strong character fantasy, prototype natural controls, choose immersive cameras, balance content, amplify the impact with game feel.
- Hard as text: the slide is a static wheel, and the phase grouping on 56 is a second diagram the learner has to map back onto the first.
- Proposal: an animated wheel. Hover a segment to read the topic and the module that teaches it; click to open that module's card on the course home. A "phases" toggle recolours the ring into Core, Readability and Insights so the two diagrams are one. Each later module page can embed the wheel with its own segment lit, as a "you are here" marker.
- Proposal detail: a third toggle, "build order", numbers the segments 1 to 7 in the notes' roadmap order and draws the path around the ring.
- Check before building: the notes name the three phases, but which ring each label sits in on slide 56 still needs to be read from the slide image.

**Target audience dials** (slides 59-62), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: identifying a target audience along three axes: Theme (light to dark), Pace (slow to fast) and Combat (gentle to harsh). Notes on 59: it is the step "many developers skip over, rushing directly to the game concept first." Notes on 60-62 walk three games from audience to insight to fantasy: Stardew Valley ("burned-out adults who loved Harvest Moon as kids", so the fantasy is "permission to slow down"), Dark Souls (players who felt modern games were "condescending", so "you are a nobody in a hostile world that owes you nothing") and Hades (players who "bounced off roguelikes because permadeath felt punishing", so "every death advances the story").
- Hard as text: three sliders on a slide can't move, and the notes' audience → insight → fantasy chain is three paragraphs per game.
- Proposal: three live sliders. The three presets snap them to Stardew Valley, Dark Souls and Hades, and a card under the dials fills in the chain: audience, insight, fantasy, then the vision choices that follow from it (Dark Souls: no difficulty settings, cryptic item text, bonfires that punish carelessness). A scatter of more games on the same axes (*Animal Crossing*, *Kirby*, *Super Mario World*, *Pokémon*, *DOOM Eternal*, *World of Warcraft*) lights up near the current dial setting, so a learner can see that neighbouring games share an audience. This previews Module 2 directly, and the same component can open that module.
- Check before building: match the three slider positions on slides 60-62 from the slide images.

**Skills & Testing / Strong Fantasy / Which characters** (slides 63-68), effort **S**, fold into the course map
- Teaches: previews of Modules 3 and 4. Notes: Celeste introduces "one verb with crystal-clear visual language" per chapter (63), Tetris "tests a single skill… that scales infinitely without changing" (64), Stray's fantasy is "a stray cat… slipping through a vast neon city built for someone else" (65), and Hitman's is a "nameless master assassin" (66). Slide 67 quotes Mohammad Alavi, "The character is the nexus of fantasy and gameplay", and slide 68 asks "Which characters do I need? How do I make them compelling?" with seven "???" slots.
- Proposal: no separate page. Slide 68's seven mystery slots can become a reveal on the course map's Character segment: seven silhouettes that fill in with League of Legends champion portraits by class (assassin, mage, tank, support, marksman, fighter, controller) when clicked. Better built in full for Module 4.

**Titanfall 2 wall-run assist** (slide 69), effort **S**, GIF
- Teaches: notes: "Respawn iterated on the 'auto-stick' threshold for weeks. Too sticky and players felt trapped against walls; too loose and they fell off mid-parkour… the moment you press jump near a wall, the game biases toward your intended direction, not the literal stick input."
- Proposal: a top-down runner beside a wall with a stick-angle arrow and an "assist" slider. At zero the runner follows the literal stick and peels off; at max it clings and can't leave; in the middle it reads intent. Three runs side by side make one GIF, and the slider could open Module 5.

**Puppet architecture signal** (slide 70), effort **S**, quick win (see above)
- Teaches: the "4 Pieces of Puppet Architecture": Controllers, Pawns, Models, Actions.
- Proposal: a button press travels Controller → Pawn → Model → Actions as a signal hop, using the same hop renderer as `understand-mechanics/shared/xray.js`. A toggle swaps the gamepad Controller for an AI Controller and the other three boxes don't change, which is the reason the split exists.

**Camera types teaser** (slides 71-73), effort **S**, low priority
- Teaches: what Module 6 covers: 11 camera types, cinematography principles, field of view and its effect on feel, and 8 optimisations for third-person follow cameras. Notes on 71: "The camera is your primary tool for telling the player what matters right now": God of War (2018)'s single unbroken over-the-shoulder shot, Hollow Knight's camera that leads during dashes, Cyberpunk 2077's first person.
- Proposal: a looping GIF of one small scene seen through those three framings, then an FOV slider sweep. Defer the full build to the Module 6 proposal.

**Same strike, polish layered on** (slides 75-78, 81), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: "Amplified Game Feel", shown through three samples the course builds: a melee strike (Slasher), an FPS sample after *Metroid Prime* (Shooter) and an on-rails space shooter. Notes on 75: game feel isn't "just 'some polish'"; its purpose is "to communicate the state of the game to the player right away." Slide 81: "Like self improvement, polish has no upper limit!"
- Hard as text: the samples are videos or builds that sit outside the deck, and "polish" is a felt difference that a learner only sees side by side.
- Proposal: one melee strike against a training dummy, played on loop. A stack of toggles adds one layer at a time: anticipation frames, hit stop, screen shake, knockback, hit flash, particles, a hit sound and a camera nudge. A meter counts the layers, and a "raw vs polished" split screen shows both at once. The GIF records the layers turning on one by one, which is slide 81's point. Module 9 can extend the same page with the shooter and space samples instead of starting over.
