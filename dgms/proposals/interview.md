This is a short talk rather than a module: who you are as a designer, what the interviewer looks for, what each seniority level sounds like, the 4Cs, and five tactics. Several slides are built-up lists or images whose content isn't in the text (the hiring process funnel on 2, the sub-disciplines image on 6, the levels image on 36), and many notes say "\*Adlib\*" or "\*read slide\*". So this deck needs fewer widgets than the modules, and more of the value is in giving the talk a fixed, reusable form: a role map, a level ladder and a practice rubric that learners can come back to before a real interview.

## Know yourself and apply accordingly (slides 4-28)

**Which designer are you? Role map** (slides 7-27), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: notes on 5: design roles are "often times… specialized such as systems, combat, mission, etc. which require discipline specific skills." The first split is content vs systems (8). Content design is "the umbrella term for all the sub-disciplines that fill the game with content such as quests, monsters, NPCs" and focuses "on the quality of an individual piece of the game experience" (9). Examples (10): League of Legends champion and skin designers, Legends of Runeterra card designers, WoW's quest, raid, class, item and combat content designers, match-3 puzzle designers, and Call of Duty's mission and enemy designers. Systems design is "designing the harmonious interactions between different elements of the game loop" (11). The grid then fills in by domain (12-23): interactions (mechanics designer vs puzzle designer; notes on 13: "great combat design is… a real time combat puzzle"), space and pace (character and controller vs level; notes on 16: "Character and control = systems to navigate spaces, often built inside gymnasiums"), usability (UX vs UI), plot and story (narrative vs quest/mission), challenges (technical vs combat; notes on 21: technical designers build "gyms, zoos, and museums" and bridge engineers and mechanics designers, and "People joke that Technical Designers are 'technically designers'"), and finance (economy, monetization). A second grid places four types on systems/experience and concrete/abstract axes: Director, Architect, Empath and Analyst (8, 12, 24). Only the Analyst is explained: "Analysts adjust specific values within the systems to create a great experience, often after the empath has created it" (24). The mechanics designer's responsibilities (14, 27): rules and behaviours of gameplay elements, balance and meaningful choices, iterating from playtests.
- Hard as text: the grid is built up across nine slides, one row at a time, with role pairs on separate slides. It's a table that was never shown whole.
- Proposal: one table, domains as rows (interactions, space and pace, usability, plot and story, challenges, finance) and two columns (systems-leaning, content-leaning). Each cell opens a short card with the role's description from the notes and the studio examples from slide 10 (League and WoW examples can use their approved icons). Each card links to the course modules that train that role: the mechanics designer card points to Modules 3-5 and 8, the character and controller card to Modules 4-6, and so on, so the map doubles as a guide through the course. A second tab draws the Director/Architect/Empath/Analyst quadrant with a few self-check questions per axis.
- Source for the quadrant: the four types come from Richard Carrillo's framework, in his book *The Role of a Great Game Designer* (2021) and his GDC 2015 talk "Designing Your Design Team" (gdcvault.com/play/1021922). The GDC listing says the talk covers "the four major types, how to identify them, and how to determine their skill level… which are leaders and which are doers." Credit Carrillo on the quadrant tab.
- The four types, as Xelnath supplied them (2026-11-05). In Carrillo's updated book, "Visionary" became "Director" and "Salesman" became "Architect"; the deck uses the updated names:
  - **Director** (formerly "Visionary", confirmed by Xelnath): high-level direction, world-building, narrative and thematic fantasy. Anchors "the emotional or conceptual soul of the project", defining what the game wants to say or make the player feel.
  - **Architect** (formerly "Salesman"): market appeal, high-concept hooks and engagement value. Champions the pitch and immediate excitement, so the game stands out and communicates its value clearly.
  - **Empath**: the player-centric experience, moment-to-moment fun and accessibility. Looks at the game through the player's eyes, tuning control feel, pacing, emotional highs and lows, and comfort.
  - **Analyst**: systems, math, balance and long-term progression. Builds the economies, combat math and spreadsheets that keep the rules fair and deep over time. This matches the deck's own note on 24 ("Analysts adjust specific values within the systems… often after the empath has created it").
- Check before building: which quadrant each type sits in on the deck's systems/experience and concrete/abstract axes (the slide image places them, but the text export doesn't say where). The self-check questions per type can then be written from the definitions above.

## How to approach interviews (slides 29-47)

**One question, five levels** (slides 35-41), effort **S**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: what each level sounds like in an interview: "Junior - doesn't know the answer but can talk it out", "Mid - has answers and knows how to execute", "Senior - can execute independently & thinks about the organizational costs", "Lead - can delegate and supervise mids+", "Director - can broadcast vision across multiple disciplines clearly".
- Proposal: one design prompt (for example, "players say the dodge feels unreliable; what do you do?") with five written answers, one per level, revealed by a level slider. Each answer is highlighted where it shows its level's trait (talking it out, a concrete plan, a cost to other teams, who does what, a shared vision). The learner writes their own answer and tags which traits it shows. Short and reusable with different prompts.

**Interview rubric** (slides 29-34, 42-47), effort **S**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: what the interviewer cares about: "Do you explore the problem before jumping in? Do you consider multiple possible solution paths? Do you understand the tradeoffs and costs? Are you a collaborator?" (31-34). What they screen for: the 4Cs, critical thinking, communication, creativity and consistency (43-47). Notes on 25: "Once I know what kind of designer you are… I want to see how curious you are! Curiosity goes far beyond just ability to know games."
- Proposal: a printable rubric card with the four interviewer questions and the 4Cs as rows, each with a one-line "looks like" example. It pairs with the practice card below, where learners score themselves after a mock answer.

## Quick ways to improve your odds (slides 48-52)

**Practice card** (slides 48-52), effort **M**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: five tactics: "If you need time, ask for it!", "Make it a collaboration", "Ask what they've already tried", "Connect to your history", "Practice, practice, practice" (49). "You have some control over the interview! Try to steer the interview to the topic you are strongest" (50). Frameworks "are repeatable tools", "Organize your thoughts", "Help you handle things on the fly" and "Improve consistency of design thinking" (52).
- Proposal: a prompt generator that draws a design question (tied to the role picked on the role map), starts a timer, and shows the five tactics as a checklist beside it. After the answer, the learner scores themselves on the rubric. A "frameworks" drawer lists the course's own frameworks to reach for (Module 1's pillars, Module 3's skill tests, Module 8's balance checklist, Module 9's seven principles), which makes slide 52's point concrete: the course is the framework library.

**The hiring funnel** (slides 2-3), effort **S**, **TOP 5**
- Teaches: notes on 2: "Go over the first three steps lightly. Mention that the first two happen before an interview even occurs… it's generally these steps especially for AA and AAA studios." - Proposal: a funnel graphic with each stage as a band and a one-line tip on each. The webcam tips sit on the phone screen band.
