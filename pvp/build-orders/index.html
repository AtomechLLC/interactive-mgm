Build Orders

Rush, Turtle and Expand are bets. A build order is how you place one: every worker, every building, every second, in order. Write one, run it, and watch the bet pay off or go bust.

Counter Cycles drew the RTS opening triangle as a wheel. This module opens the wheel up: each opening is a list of things to build in order, and a small simulation turns that list into a clock. The faction here is original, with timings in the spirit of a classic sci-fi RTS opening: 12 workers and 50 ore at 0:00, workers mine about 57 ore a minute, buildings need a worker to put them up, and supply caps how much you can field.

Reading a Build Order

Players write builds as supply count + what to build. The number is when: “place it the moment your supply hits 13”. Hover a line.

    The standard opening, written the way players write it. Each line is a supply count and a building. Workers are never on the list: making them nonstop is assumed, which is why the numbers keep climbing between lines. Hover a line to jump the clock to that moment and light its bar on the timeline.

    Build Order Simulator

    Your build

      Click the tech tree to add a step after the highlighted line (or at the end). Greyed nodes are locked: nothing earlier in your build unlocks them yet. Drag lines to reorder, × to remove. The simulation reruns on every change and prints the supply count each step actually started at, which is the build written in standard notation. Coloured chips on a line say what it waited for: ore, gas, supply (a supply block) or tech. Workers are made nonstop up to the worker cap; after your last step the toggles can keep every building producing, add Supply Posts and spend floating ore on more production, the way a player would.

      Three Ways to Lose

      Same plan, same clock. Each toggle breaks one habit. The faint line is the clean build.

      Build orders are easy to read and hard to execute. Three classic leaks: a supply block (production stops because there’s no room), cutting workers (the economy flattens forever, not just for a moment), and floating ore (money in the bank is an army that doesn’t exist). Stack them to see how quickly a good plan turns into a losing one.

      One Clock, Three Openings

      attacker
      defender
      Run Rush, Turtle and Expand side by side and ask, at every second: if this player attacked that one now, who wins? Defenders fight at home with Bunkers and a 20% home advantage. Fights use Lanchester’s square law: strength is power squared, so a bigger army wins by more than its margin and keeps most of itself. Scrub the clock and watch the matrix: early, Rush beats Expand and Turtle holds Rush. Late, Expand outgrows Turtle. The counter cycle isn’t a rule someone wrote; it falls out of the build orders and the clock.

      Played Out on the Map

      attacker
      defender
      The same simulation, drawn as a game. Every worker, building and unit appears on the map the moment the build order makes it: workers mine crystals and gas, buildings go up where they’re placed, soldiers gather at the top of the ramp (or at the second base once it’s finished). The chips under the map are each build order, ticking off as it runs; the number is the supply count the step actually started at. At the chosen time the attacker’s whole army moves out. The fight is the One Clock slide’s, with one change: the defender gets the time the attack takes to walk across the map, so its army is counted when the attack lands, not when it leaves. Best picks the first moment that attack wins; if it never does, it shows the closest try. A winning attack then raids the nearest base’s workers. Yours plays the build from the Simulator slide.

        What a Build Order Is

        A bet, written down

        Every opening spends the same ore on different futures: army now, safety now, or economy later. The order is the bet.

        A clock

        Supply counts are timestamps. A build is a schedule, and a supply block or an idle bank is a missed appointment.

        A counter cycle, in time

        Rush beats Expand before it pays off; Expand beats Turtle after. The wheel is the clock, folded.

        Counter Cycles: the RTS wheel →
        For a designer the takeaway is the tuning surface: build times, costs, supply and travel all decide when each opening peaks. Move one number (say, the Bunker’s cost) and the window where Rush beats Turtle opens or closes. A healthy counter cycle needs those windows to exist and to overlap.
