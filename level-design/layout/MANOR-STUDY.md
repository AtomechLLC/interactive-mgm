# The manor plan: a measured study

Working notes behind the slide "From a hallway to a manor" (`manor.js`). The subject is the fourth of four reference floor plans, ordered from a very junior designer to a very senior one. The reference image is not in this repository and the slide's plan is our own composition; this file records what was measured on the reference and the rules drawn from it.

## How it was measured

The reference is a small raster, so every figure here is an estimate. Wall directions come from line detection on the image, widths from pixel counts across walls. The scale is an assumption: the stair treads are taken to be 0.29 m deep, which makes one source pixel about 5.8 cm. Door openings then come out between 0.65 m and 1.0 m, which is believable, so the scale is probably right to within 15 percent. Proportions and angles do not depend on it.

## What the drawing is made of

**Three grids, no more.** Every straight wall runs on one of three directions or at a right angle to one of them.

| Grid | Turn | Who uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main | 0° | Hall, tower's flat faces, loggia, apse, outbuilding (about 26% of all wall length lies on the main horizontal alone) |
| North-east wing | about +18° | The wing and the stair inside it |
| South-west wing | about −22° | The wing, the porch, the stair band (which reads about −17°) and the outside stair |

There is no 45° wall anywhere. The turns are small and unequal, and they go opposite ways.

**Five kinds of boundary.** The drawing uses line weight as a vocabulary.

| Boundary | Drawn width | Real size | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heavy wall | 6.5 px | 0.38 m | The tower's arc only |
| Standard wall | 4.5 px | 0.26 m | Hall, wings, apse |
| Thin wall | 2.5 px | 0.15 m | Loggia, porch, stoop walls, part of the outbuilding |
| Pier line | dashes of standard width | piers about 0.3 m, gaps 0.5 to 0.9 m | Between nave and aisle, hall and loggia, hall and cross-aisle |
| Level edge | thin grey | a step or a tread | Stairs, the tower's tiers |

The same plan drawn in a single weight would lose most of its meaning. The heavy arc says the tower is the oldest, most solid part. Thin walls mark the parts that are nearly outdoors. Piers divide a room without closing it.

## What the plan is doing

**1. Volumes overlap; they do not sit side by side.** The tower's square corner pushes into the hall, and the lower hall wraps around it. The north-east wing's wall slices through the hall and cuts the aisle into a wedge. A junior plan places rooms next to each other and joins them with doors. Here each volume takes a bite out of its neighbour, so every room has at least one wall that belongs to something else.

**2. Each volume is square to itself.** Inside the north-east wing every wall is at +18° or at right angles to that. Nothing is skewed for effect. The irregular shapes appear only where two regular things collide. This is the main difference from "avoid right angles": the plan is full of right angles, on three different grids.

**3. Wings hinge on corners.** Both wings pivot about a corner of the hall, not the middle of a wall. That keeps the hall's long walls intact and puts the awkward geometry in one place.

**4. A change of direction is a change of level.** Every place two grids meet has a stair in it, and the stair lies along the seam instead of across it. The north-east stair runs along the wing's wall. The south-west seam is a whole band: flight, landing room, flight. The player turns on a landing, so the new direction arrives with a new height and a new view.

**5. Leftover wedges are put to work.** Colliding grids leave triangles. Here they become the aisle bay, the stair band, the porch and the outside stair. No sliver is left as dead space.

**6. Rooms are carved, not just outlined.** The north-east wing is a rectangle with an L-shaped partition; the south-west wing is an L with return walls at its door. From any doorway you see part of the room and a corner to go round.

**7. No door faces a door.** Every sequence of openings is offset, so crossing a room means a diagonal or a turn. There is no straight shot through the house.

**8. Enclosure comes in a gradient.** Heavy tower, standard hall and wings, piers, thin loggia and porch, then the open yard. Moving outwards, the walls get lighter one stage at a time.

**9. Curves are rationed and related.** There are two: the tower and the apse. The apse's radius is about a third of the tower's, and it sits where the grids hinge, so the small round answers the big one.

**10. Routes loop through the outdoors.** The outbuilding stands off the house on the main grid, which makes the gap a yard. The wing's yard door, the outside stair and the outbuilding's doors all open onto it, so there is more than one way between most pairs of rooms, and some of those ways go outside.

**11. Sizes come in a hierarchy.** One big room (the hall, split into nave and aisle), two medium wings, then a run of small pieces. Nothing is the same size as its neighbour.

## The third plan, for contrast

The third plan uses one grid plus a single 45° chamfer, echoed by one parallel wall inside. Its idea is wrapping: a wide gallery, about 2.0 to 2.2 m, runs around two sides of a core room and is entered at the far end, so the player circles the core before entering it. It has two wall weights, a thin-walled annex, stairs deep inside and one rounded inner corner. It is a competent plan with one strong move. The fourth plan has about ten moves that all support each other.

## Recipes: from a hallway to the manor

Each recipe is one rule with numbers and a check. The slide plays them in this order.

1. **Hallway.** One corridor, 1.5 m by 6 m, a door at each end. Check: one grid, one wall weight, no loops.
2. **Swell it into a hall.** Widen to about 5.5 m. Divide it lengthwise at roughly 55/45 with piers and a single step, never a wall. Move the doors off axis. Check: two spaces, one room.
3. **Anchor a corner with a heavy round.** Radius about 45 percent of the hall's width. Push its centre inside the hall's corner so about a quarter of the circle is squared off into the hall. Draw the arc one and a half times as thick as other walls. Doors go on the flat faces only. Let the hall wrap the squared corner. Check: the tower takes a bite from the hall.
4. **Hinge a wing on the far corner.** Rotate a 4 × 6.5 m rectangle 15° to 22° about the hall's corner so its long wall cuts into the hall. Keep the wing's own walls square to each other. Check: the aisle is now a wedge, and there is a second grid.
5. **Hinge a second wing the other way.** At the diagonally opposite corner, rotate by a different amount in the opposite sense, and make this one an L. Check: three grids, no two turns equal, none of them 45°.
6. **Put the stairs in the seams.** Wherever two grids meet, lay a flight along the seam, 1.5 m wide, 6 to 8 treads. If the seam is long, make it flight, landing, flight. Then carve each wing with one partition so it reads as an L. Check: every change of direction is also a change of level.
7. **Thin the edges and round the hinge.** Add a 1.1 m loggia behind piers and a thin-walled porch on the outer faces. Put an apse, a third of the tower's radius, where the grids hinge, with a narrow door off a cross-aisle. Check: five boundary weights, walls lighter towards the outside.
8. **Close a yard.** Stand an outbuilding of two unequal bays 3 to 5 m off the house on the main grid. Open the wing, an outside stair and the outbuilding onto the gap. Check: count loops (connections minus spaces plus one). The slide ends at six.

Checks that apply at every step: no door faces a door; no two neighbouring spaces are the same size; no wedge is left unused.

## Where the first attempt fell short

The earlier version of this slide rotated rooms and chamfered corners, then joined them with doors. It had angles without grids, a single wall weight, stairs placed inside rooms instead of on seams, and volumes that touched but never overlapped. It looked irregular; the reference is regular in three directions at once.

## Still uncertain

- The direction of travel on the south-west stair band cannot be read from the image.
- The nested L lines in the tower are read as tiered steps; they could be a gallery edge.
- The porch openings and the outbuilding's inner partitions are too small to read with confidence.
- All real-world sizes rest on the 0.29 m tread assumption.

## The rules as a generator

`../shared/manorgen.js` starts every plan from a recipe and then has to deliver it. A recipe says what the manor must contain: the hall (nave and one aisle, nave and two aisles, a square great hall, or a nave with a transept), one to four towers, one to three wings and the shape of each (a two-room bar, an L, a T, or a wing wrapped round a court), whether there is a walled garden, and which edge rooms it has (loggia, apse, porch, outbuilding). `recipe(seed, asked)` keeps anything you ask for and rolls the rest.

`generate()` builds the recipe from parts, each one a rule from the study above:

- The tower's size sets the hall. The heavy round tower nests in the corner between the hall and its lower hall, and both of its doors sit on its flat faces.
- Each wing hinges on a hall corner and turns onto its own grid by 14 to 24 degrees. The turns are unequal and never all one way. The wedge the turn opens belongs to the hall, and a stair lies in that seam or at the wing's head.
- Extra towers are lesser turrets, about half the big tower's radius, on outside corners. The apse stays at a third.
- The garden, porch, loggia and outbuilding use thin walls, so the plan gets lighter toward its edge.
- Doors are not at fixed positions. The ones that join the parts are cut first; the rest are picked from the free outside walls until the routes loop, and a door is never placed straight across a room from another.
- Room sizes are nudged while building so that neighbours do not come out the same size.

About one plan in ten is the study plan itself (the fixed arrangement measured above), so the original stays in the family.

`check(plan)` reads only the finished geometry and scores fifteen rules. Three that used to describe one arrangement are now stated as the principle behind them: "one main grid, one more for each wing" (was: three grids), "turns small, unequal, not all one way" (was: two opposite turns), and "one big curve, the others clearly smaller" (was: two curves, one a third of the other). The main grid is found from the tower's flat faces, not from which grid has the most wall. The fifteenth rule, "delivers the recipe", counts towers, wings and named rooms in the drawing and compares them with what was asked.

`node level-design/layout/manor-smoke.js 1000` reports, for a thousand seeds: how often each rule holds on a first draft (84.6% pass all fifteen; the old single arrangement scored 85.8% on fourteen), that every seed reaches a passing plan with redraws, how much variety comes out (802 different recipes, 630 different arrangements and 888 different room graphs, where the old generator scored 1 of each), that all 240 recipes asked for directly are delivered, and that four rules broken on purpose are each caught.

What it still does not do: the hall is always one rectangle with the tower on one of its corners, wings attach to the hall and never to each other, and nothing here judges whether a plan is fun to play.
